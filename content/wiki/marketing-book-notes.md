# Building a Story Brand

  - The human brain is drawn towards clarity and away from confusion
  - Focus on ideas and offers in Maslow’s Hierarchy
  - Make sure messages don’t make people burn too many “mental calories”
  - The key is what you don’t say; noise is the enemy

  

1.  **\*\*A Character\*\***

<!-- end list -->

  - A story starts when a hero (the prospective customer) wants something
  - Identify something the customer/viewer wants and communicate it simply; this adds direction and definition into the story we brought them into
  - Identifying a potential desire your customer can fulfill open a story gap

\-\[example\] moviegoers watch a movie because they wonder how the story gap will close

  - Attention rises and falls with the opening and closing of the story gap
  - Now we have to bring that desire/story gap/customer ambition into one singular focus
  - Communicate your message in a way that has to do with your customer’s ability to survive and thrive, meaning to intertwine Maslow’s Hierarchy of needs

\-Maslow’s Hierarchy (bottom to top): physiological (food, water, shelter), safety (financial stability, health), belonging and love, esteem (human desire for self-worth, accomplishment, and respect from others), cognitive (knowledge and curiosity), aesthetic (appreciation of beauty and form), self-actualization (drive to realize one's full potential, talents, and capabilities) , transcendence (transcend beyond themselves, service/spirituality)

  - Our customer/viewer is a hitch-hiker and has one burning question on their mind: Where are we going? It’s our job to make sure they clearly know the answer.

<!-- end list -->

1.  **\*\*Has a Problem\*\***

<!-- end list -->

  - To gain traction for a story, a hero needs a problem, meaning we have to identify our customers/viewers problem
  - Position a villain with the problem, and our weapons we are providing could help them save their day

<!-- end list -->

1.  The villain should be the root of the problem

\-\[example\] the problem is the broken government, not high taxes

1.  Villains should be relatable; viewers should immediately recognize the villain as something they dislike
2.  Villains should be singular; 1 is plenty
3.  Villains should be real; don’t be a fear mongerer, but at the same time the villain doesn’t have to be serious

\-\[example\] the red sox are the villain on Friday for Yankees fans

  - There are 3 levels of problems that work together and draw attention

<!-- end list -->

1.  External Problems

\-what you are physically/tangibly fixing

\-\[example\] plumber fixing a leaky pipe

1.  Internal Problems

\-companies tend to sell solutions to external problem, but people buy solutions to internal problems

\-the purpose of external problems is only to manifest internal problems

\-the customer’s internal problem is what the external problem they are dealing with is causing them to feel

\-\[example\] Apple began by finding people that were intimidated by computers and wanted a friendlier interface with technology

1.  Philosophical Problems (why does the story matter in the frame of humanity?)

\-humans want to be involved in a story that is larger than themselves (transcendence on Maslow’s hierarchy)

\-what’s the deeper meaning; \[example\] “bad people shouldn’t be allowed to win”, “good vs. evil”

  - The product/service being marketed should be the single solution to the external, internal, and philosophical problem

1.  **\*\*Ands Meets a Guide\*\***

<!-- end list -->

  - Everyone is looking for a guide that has more knowledge than them and can help them solve their problems
  - A BIG MISTAKE is positioning the brand instead of the customer as the hero; the brand is the guide
  - The guide must possess both empathy and competency
  - Empathy means showing the customer we care about them the same way we care about ourselves
  - Customers look for brands/guides they have something in common with; when they realize they do, they fill their cognitive dissonance with trust, instead of thinking in details they think in chunks
  - Guides need to show brands that they are competent

<!-- end list -->

1.  Testimonials, a couple short, concise pieces that have clarity and talks about the brand and product they are solving
2.  Simple statistics
3.  Awards if possible
4.  Sinatra Test, we helped this big brand or did this big accomplishment, so if we can make it with them we can make it anywhere
5.  Press Mentions, featured by ... (endorsements, social media influencers, etc.)
6.  Logos of Other Businesses Helped, (another form of Sinatra Test)

<!-- end list -->

  - A person must gain trust (empathy and competency) to continue

1.  **\*\*Who Gives Them a Plan\*\***

<!-- end list -->

  - At this point, the customer may see our brand as the guide. They may trust our brand. They have a problem. But to solve that problem they need to cross a creek. They want to get to the solution on the other side, but they hear a waterfall downstream. What happens if they fall in? What does life look like if they fall in the waterfall? They are risking putting their skin and hard earned dollars on the line. At this point, when you ask them to buy they think “What if it doesn’t work? What if I’m a fool for buying this? How long will it take to work? What if I buy it and never use it again? How do I get from where I am to the place where I’ve adopted this product in my life? The customer’s life is as unstable as it is familiar. A new product means change, which is difficult, and often a problem they want to keep moving to the next day. They think, “I’ve got this problem I need to solve, but I can probably live with it a little longer, so maybe I will just deal with finding the solution tomorrow or the next day. Our customer is currently stuck on the wrong side of the creek, but the shore they are on is dry, so why even bother to cross the creek at all? In order to ease concerns and cognitive dissonance (mental discomfort experienced when holding two or more contradictory beliefs), we need to place large stones in the rushing creek our customer faces.
  - Instead of saying, “do you want to purchase …”, say “the process of buying ‘x’ product from us is 5 steps …”
  - All successful plans do 2 things well ...

<!-- end list -->

1.  Clarifies what doing business with us will look like
2.  Remove the sense of risk a customer might feel before committing to a product/service

<!-- end list -->

  - “If you confuse, you lose”, not having a plan is the easiest way to create cognitive dissonance in potential customers
  - After hearing a pitch, speech, or viewing a website, all customers wonder the same thing … “What would my next steps be if I wanted to move forward?” If we don’t clearly guide them with next steps, the customer experiences a little confusion, and because they can't hear the waterfall downstream, they use that confusion as an excuse not to do business with us
  - There are 2 types of plans we can use ... 

<!-- end list -->

1.  The Process plan

\-describes the steps a customer needs to take to buy a product, steps they need to use after buying a product, or both

\-this plan is ideally 3-5 steps long

\-\[example\] 1. Test a car 2. Purchase a car 3. Enjoy free lifetime maintenance

1.  The Agreement Plan (about alleviating fears)

\-a list of arguments you make with your customers to help them overcome fears of doing business with you

\-often happens in the background; \[example\] Whole Foods values work as an agreement to source food in a way that is socially and environmentally responsible

\-best way to make an agreement plan is to list all the things your customer may be concerned about as it relates to your product/service and then counter that list with agreements that alleviate those concerns

  - Once the plan is made, whether it is a process, agreement, or both, give each plan a title and formalize it in the minds of customers; \[example\] “World’s Best Night Sleep Plan”

1.  **\*\*And Calls Them to Action\*\***

<!-- end list -->

  - Characters never take action on their own in stories; they have to be challenged to do so; no story has ever been about a guy wanting to climb Mt. Everest then one day looking in the mirror and deciding to do it; people follow the scientific principle, bodies at rest tend to stay at rest, and so do our heroes
  - Keep in mind customers are bombarded with over 3,000 commercial messages daily, so unless our brand is bold in our call to action, we will be ignored
  - Make sure to not put customers in the mind reading business; communicate clearly because people can’t infer what you are trying to say
  - When it is time to place an order, sellers often say, “If you are interested, let me know.” This is too passive. A customer will subconsciously think, “Am I interested? I don’t know. Why won’t you contact me?”; the worst thing that can happen is that a customer is convinced and ready to place the order but thinks, “This guy wants me to contact him later. I hope I remember to do that.”
  - Instead, next time when it is time to close the order say, “If you are struggling with ‘x’, which it sounds like you are, I think buying ‘y’ will be the right decision for you. I can take your order now and you won’t need to struggle with ‘x’ anymore. Would you like me to place your order now?”
  - Be clear with the buy now or call to action and make sure to clearly (colors, font, size, maybe a button?) include it in the marketing material
  - You are never as pushy as you think you are when you are asking customers to buy, that is your job to ask them to

1.  Transitional Calls to Action (used as a ramp up to build trust with potential customers not quite ready to order, but will help lead to an eventual purchase)

\-a good transitional call to action does the following ... 

1.  Stakes claim to a territory before beat by the competition

1.  Create Reciprocity

\-the more generous a brand is willing to give, the more generous a customer is willing to give back in the future

1.  Continually position yourself as the guide

\-when you help your customer solve a problem, even for free, they view you as the expert and will return to you for future issues in that area of expertise

\-\[examples\] PDF, videos, podcasts, webinars, live events, testimonials, samples, risk-free trials

# Obviously Awesome

**Positioning in Context**

  - Even a world class product not positioned right can fail
  - Positioning completely changes how consumers think about a product

\-”If you’re a baker making bread, you’re a baker. If you make the best bread in the world, you’re not an artist, but if you bake the bread in the gallery, you’re an artist.” Context Matters

  - A lot of people market a product in the most obvious market, but don’t realize products can be positioned in multiple markets

\-Trap 1: You’re stuck on the idea of what you intended to build, and you don’t realize your product has become something else

\-Trap 2: You carefully designed your product for a market, but that market has changed

  

**The 5 Components of Effective Positioning**

1.  Competitive Alternatives

\-what customers would do if your product/service didn’t exist

\-this is often times not purchasing the product/service from your competitors

1.  Unique Attributes 

\-the features and capabilities that you have and the alternatives lack

1.  Value (and proof)

\-the benefit that those features enable for customers

1.  Target Market Characteristics

\-the traits of a group of buyers that led them to really care a lot about the value you deliver

\-this is your ideal customer

1.  Market Category

\-the market you describe yourself as being a part of, which gives consumers greater context about the specific value you provide

\-this triggers a set of assumptions amongst consumers

1.  Bonus Category: Relevant Trends

\-trends that your target customer(s) understand and/or are interested in that can help your product/service gain relevance at the current moment

  
  

**The 10 Step Positioning Process**

1.  Understand the customers who love your product

<!-- end list -->

  - Your best fit customers should be the key to understanding what the value in the product is

\-Step 1: make a list of your best fit customers; if you don’t have any yet, test the product/service on a variety of audiences and see what patterns emerge

\-START WITH THE CUSTOMERS, NOT THE PRODUCT

\-You do not position the same way to customers and investors

  - If you have a single product/service company, try to position your company and product the same
  - If you have a multiproduct/multiservice company, try to focus on one product positioning if you have one killer product, or whole company positioning if your products/services sell equally

<!-- end list -->

1.  The Positioning Team

<!-- end list -->

  - Positioning impacts multiple groups among large organizations
  - This means 1-2 reps from each of the following groups should contribute to meetings on the topic because of their varied perspectives on the company and customers

\-Marketing (messaging, audience targeting, campaign development)

\-Sales & Business Dev. (target customer segmentation and account strategy (deciding how to approach each kind of customer) )

\-Customer Success (onboarding & account expansion strategy (can we upgrade them, raise the price, sell more seats) )

\-Product Developers (makers, roadmaps, prioritization)

1.  Let go on your previous thoughts on positioning this product/service

<!-- end list -->

  - If we always thought the product belongs in one market, it’s hard to imagine it belonging in another market that might suit it better
  - Products/services can mean many different things to buyers
  - Market confusion is created when there is a disconnect in the perception of the value of the product between the product creators and the customers

<!-- end list -->

1.  List your true competitive alternatives

<!-- end list -->

  - Customers don’t always see competitors the same way we do and their opinion is the only one that matters
  - Our product features and value are only unique, interesting, and valuable when a competitor perceives them in relation to alternatives
  - What would be your customer's replacement for your product/service if you didn’t exist? What do your customers identify as your alternative solutions?

\-This could be the business next door that sells the same good, it could be just dealing with the issue and not fixing it, it could be making a solution themselves, it could be buying a whole different product instead

1.  Isolate your unique attributes and features

<!-- end list -->

  - Strong positioning is centered on what your product does best
  - Step 1 was to list all of your capabilities that the alternatives do not have
  - Your opinions of your own strengths are irrelevant without proof

\-\[example\] the statements “we provide outstanding customer service” and “very easy to use” are very bad examples; instead prove your advantages in tangible ways; \[examples\] proving training is required to use your competitors product when your’s doesn’t need any; quantify the time it takes customers to be proficient in your product compared to your competitors; third-party or customer reviews of the product/service

  - CONCENTRATE ON CONSIDERATION, NOT RETENTION ATTRIBUTES

\-consideration attributions are things the customer cares about when evaluating whether or not to make a purchase

\-retention attributes are features that aren't important to making the initial purchase, but are important when it comes time to renew the purchase

1.  Map attributes to value themes

<!-- end list -->

  - Features can have multiple value points, and a combination of features can provide value as well
  - Make a table by listing all your features in the first column, then list all the benefits of each of those features in the second column, then list all the value of each of those benefits in the third column
  - Group the values into themes based on patterns and commonalities
  - The goal of this is not to highlight every feature, it’s about highlighting the most important and special features or combination of features

<!-- end list -->

1.  Determine who cares a lot

<!-- end list -->

  - Target your market segmentation (best-fit customers) as narrow as possible to reach enough customers to hit the company’s sales goals; we can expand the range of a fitting customer later
  - The market segment targeted must have common, specific, unmet needs

\-\[example\] a tennis racket that has a thing on the bottom to help seniors with knee problems pick up the ball

1.  Find a market frame of reference that puts your strengths at the center and determine how to position it

<!-- end list -->

  - Use abductive reasoning (working backwards to make an assumption)

\-based on isolating key features, value, and asking what type of products/market fit these categories

  - Examining adjacent or growing markets
  - Ask your customers (with caution)

\-they have limited or sometimes biased perspectives and knowledge

  - The different approaches for choosing & entering a market are ... 

<!-- end list -->

1.  Aim to be the leader of a market category

\-The goal is to beat the leader at their own game and convince customers your company has the best solution

\-This is rarely the case for small/new companies unless the leader is at a disadvantage or there is no defined leader;

\-your product must be inarguably new from those in other market categories, not just merely a new feature added

\-timing is very important when creating a new game; many consumers will ask, “Why do we need this now?”

\-sell the market on the problem first, not the solution

  - Aligning current product positioning with a relevant trend can make it look current and relevant

  - Need to declare a market and connect the trend to the product to use one

# Made to Stick

  - In 1990, Elizabeth Newton earned her PH.D. from Stanford in psychology by studying a simple game. She gave people one of two roles: “tappers” or “listeners”. Tappers were given a list of 25 well known songs like Happy Birthday To You and The Star Spangled Banner. Each tapper had to pick out a song and tap it for the listener, who had to guess the song based on the taps. Over the course of Newton’s experiment, 120 songs were tapped out. Listeners guessed 3/120 songs correctly (2.5%). Before they guessed though, the tappers predicted the odds of their corresponding listener to guess the song correctly to be 50%. When a tapper taps, they hear the song in their head. Listeners can’t. When a listener guessed Happy Birthday To You for the Star Spangled Banner, tappers said, “How stupid can you be.” The problem is, tappers are given the song title, so it’s impossible for them to imagine what it’s like to lack knowledge. They can’t imagine what it’s like to hear isolated taps instead of a song. This is what we call the CURSE OF KNOWLEDGE. Once we know something, it’s so hard to imagine what it was like to lack that knowledge. That’s the barrier we must overcome.
  - In 1999, an Israeli research team assembled 200 highly regarded ads that were finalists for awards in top advertising competitions. They found that 89% of award winning ads could be classified into 6 categories. They tried to use those same 6 templates to classify 200 other ads, from the same publication that did not receive awards. They could only classify 2% of those ads with the frameworks. The lesson: highly creative ads are more predictable than uncreative ones. Can novices do this too? The Israeli researchers tested this. They brought in 3 groups of novices. They were all given background info on 3 products: shampoo, a diet food, and a sneaker. One group immediately started making ads without training. The second group was trained for two hours by an experienced creative instructor. The participants were shown a free association brainstorming method, which was a standard creativity method. The final group was trained for 6 hours on how to use the 6 creativity templates. An experienced creative director, who did not know how the groups had been trained selected the top 15 ads from each group. These ads were tested on consumers. The ads from the first group were described as “annoying”. The second group were no more creative, but less annoying. The ads from the third group had 50% more creativity and 55% more positive attitude toward the product being advertised. It appears there are systematic ways to creativity.

  - The Hawaii Trip

\-The final exam results for some college students come back in two days

\-The students were given 3 options

1.  Buy the vacation to Hawaii for a bargain price today
2.  Pass on the vacation
3.  Pay $5 to lock in the price for 2 more days

\-57% of students who were told they passed chose to go and 54% of students who were told they failed also chose to go

\-Out of the students who did not know their scores, 61% paid $5 to lock in the price, despite being willing to make the purchase regardless of their score

\-This is the Disjunction Effect: People avoid making a decision under uncertainty even when their choice would be the same in every possible outcome

  - College Choices

\-Students were told that they had 2 choices one evening

1.  Attend a lecture by an author you admire that is visiting for the evening
2.  Go to the library to study

\-21% chose to go study and 79% chose to go to the lecture

\-Researchers now gave a different group of students 3 choices

1.  Attend a lecture by an author you admire that is visiting for the evening
2.  Go to the library to study
3.  Watch a film that you’ve been waiting to see

\-40% of people chose to go study in this scenario

\-Decoy Effect: A carefully added option can steer people toward a specific choice because humans judge value by comparison

\-The same thing happens when subscription pricing gives 3 options

1.  Online only $59
2.  Print only $125
3.  Print and Online $125 

<!-- end list -->

They will have an anxiety on what to choose, sometimes between two good choices like the lecture and the film. Prioritization helps people avoid bad choices by reminding them about what’s important.

  - The Pomelo Schema

\-Multiple explanations were given to get people to understand what a Pomelo was. Which explanation led you to understanding more?

  - African Donation Paradox

\-CMU professors set up a survey; each participant gets an envelope with ‘5’ $1 bills and 1 of 2 letters

\-the 1st letter contained multiple statistics about the magnitude of the problem in Africa; \[example\] “food shortages in Malawiare are affecting more than 3 million children”

\-the 2nd letter stated, “any money that you donate will go to Rokia, a 7 year old girl from Mali Africa …” finishing off with the hardships she is dealing with

\-participants were either given the 1st or 2nd request letter and left alone; participants could send the letter they received back if they’d like

\-on average, $1.14 was donated to the first letter and $2.38 was donated to the 2nd letter

\-when it comes to our hearts, one individual trumps the masses

\-smaller donations to the first letter could also potentially be explained by the “drop in the bucket” effect (people feeling overwhelmed by the scale & thought their donations would be meaningless)

\-so researchers brought a 3rd group who got both letters; this group gets the stats and story combo so theoretically they should get more donations? Well this group only got $1.43 on average

\-why? thinking about stats makes people analytical, and think less emotionally, and thinking emotionally led to people taking more action in the case of the 2nd letter

\-a 2nd study was run with different people

\-group 1 were asked “if an object travels at five feet per minute, then calculate how many feet it will travel in 360 seconds?”

\-group 2 were asked “please write down one word to describe how you feel when you hear the word ‘baby’”

\-both groups were then given Rokia’s letter; group 1 donated an average of $1.26, group 2 donated an average of $2.34
